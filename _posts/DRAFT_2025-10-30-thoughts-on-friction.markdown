I recently started a blog called [Stone Tools](https://stonetools.ghost.io). In it, I re-examine productivity software of the so-called "golden age" of computing. I cover software like VisiCalc and Deluxe Paint on systems like the Apple 2 and Commodore Amiga. Some systems seem to hold a vice-like grip on a particular segment of the computing hobbyist crowd, and I'm no exception.

I'm looking at the older software both to understand WHY that grip is so strong, and also to see if it still offers something of value to users of today. I recently posted a story about CAD-3D on the Atari ST. In the article I touch for a moment on the concept of "friction" in the user experience, and I wanted to expand those thoughts into a more considered essay.

The primary thesis I posit in that article is, "Without friction, there can be no spark."

Humans seem to thrive under constraints. 

We need that moment of overcoming an obstacle, or being told something is impossible. When a computer of the past presented such a challenge, there were many of us who said, "Fuck you, you don't tell me what to do." and proceeded to prove the computer wrong. Some did it through programming. I did it in Deluxe Paint. "Only 32 colors? Fuck you, I'll get more than that by alternating two colors to mix them!" I didn't know the word "dithering" back then, but I knew I wasn't going to let the Amiga defeat my artistic goals.

I submit that the satisfaction and warm memories of overcoming the limitations of older computers, or of overcoming perceived self-limitations by way of the computer, is the heart of retrocomputing nostalgia. I will further submit that the lack of such limits in computers today is why the systems of today do not carry any particularly strong allegiance. I think you are far more likely to find a Windows 3.1 stan than for Windows 8.

I'm sure there is a big parallel here with gaming and overcoming those challenges. I would humbly suggest that the artificial, curated challenges of games are a slightly different beast. The existence of speed-running kind of supports me on this, I think. They are the "Fuck you, you don't tell me what to do" crowd of gaming. Human-on-human gaming challenges scratch the eternal "I'm the champion" itch.
